A Stronger Yuan, a Harder Choice: Why Exporters Hedge Currency Risk

A stronger currency is often described as a sign of confidence, but for exporters it can create a difficult calculus. Reuters reported on September 14 that the yuan had risen about 4.3% this year and was near a four-year high against the dollar. That appreciation improves the foreign-currency value of domestic assets, yet it can compress export margins when overseas revenue is converted back into yuan. The dilemma is most pronounced for firms with thin margins and little pricing power.

The first mechanism is accounting. An exporter may issue an invoice in dollars, record a receivable, and receive cash weeks later. If the exchange rate moves before settlement, the yuan value of that receivable can change. A payable in foreign currency creates the opposite exposure. Even without any cash loss, translation of foreign earnings can produce an unrealized change; once payment occurs, part of that change becomes realized. The denomination chosen for invoicing therefore matters as much as the sale price itself.

Hedging tries to insulate operating decisions from exchange-rate swings. A company can lock in a forward rate, use a swap to exchange cash flows, or post collateral under a contract with a counterparty. The notional amount of such contracts can be large even when the actual cash at risk is smaller. SAFE said Chinese enterprises contracted nearly $1.4 trillion of foreign-exchange derivatives in the first half of 2026, while the corporate hedging ratio reached 35.3%. The resulting turnover also helps explain why treasury teams now treat currency management as a core financial responsibility.

Yet more hedging does not mean eliminating all risk. A firm that hedges too little may suffer from appreciation; one that hedges too much can lose flexibility if the currency later moves in its favor. Hedging costs can also rise when volatility is high, while stringent collateral terms may squeeze liquidity. Managers therefore need discretion rather than a rigid directive: the objective is to reduce harmful variance, not to bet on a perfect forecast. An enforceable contract and reliable counterparty remain essential.

The market itself has several layers. Onshore yuan trading reflects domestic rules and liquidity conditions, while offshore markets allow international investors and companies to transact outside mainland China. Differences between the two can create temporary arbitrage, although transaction costs and regulation often restrain it. Convertibility is not absolute, and the gap between markets can become more discernible during stress. Greater transparency and predictable rules can reduce unnecessary friction without pretending that every market must trade at exact parity.

Currency moves also reshape corporate behavior. A stronger yuan can encourage importers by lowering the local-currency cost of foreign goods, but it can exacerbate pressure on exporters whose costs are domestic and whose sales are abroad. Some firms may divert production toward the home market, reconfigure supply chains, or accelerate the repatriation of overseas earnings. Others may leave more cash offshore, especially when the expected trajectory is unclear. These choices can gradually produce a broader reorientation of investment and trade.

Small firms face a different problem: sophistication. Large corporations may have proprietary risk systems, specialist staff, and detailed monitoring, whereas smaller exporters may lack financial literacy or access to advisory services. That gap can entrench uneven protection. Subsidizing option premiums or bank advice may help, but policy design deserves scrutiny because support can become concentrated among firms that already have strong banking relationships. The statutory basis for any subsidy should also be clear enough to preserve accountability.

There is also a macroeconomic trade-off. If many companies react to the same exchange-rate signal at once, their transactions can become procyclical: they may buy protection after the currency has already moved sharply, reinforcing market pressure. Countercyclical tools can lean against that behavior, but they should not suppress genuine demand for risk management. Policy must contend with a basic equilibrium problem—how to support orderly adjustment without freezing prices or encouraging a one-way propensity to chase recent moves.

For workers and suppliers, exchange rates matter indirectly. Margin pressure may affect investment, bonuses, and remuneration, while a sustained squeeze can alter procurement or hiring. But the effect is rarely mechanical or purely structural. Productivity gains, stronger foreign demand, or lower imported-input costs can offset some losses. The residual impact depends on each firm's export share, contract structure, and ability to pass costs through. A granular assessment is therefore more useful than a single headline estimate.

The long-term lesson is that hedging is a form of resilience, not a prediction contest. A steady framework should improve access to instruments, reporting quality, and market predictability while allowing firms to choose how much risk to retain. Revaluation or devaluation will still redistribute gains and losses, and cross-border remittance and capital flows will still respond. The best policy does not promise a fixed exchange rate; it builds institutions that can absorb transitory shocks, reduce unnecessary instability, and keep commercial decisions separate from speculation.
