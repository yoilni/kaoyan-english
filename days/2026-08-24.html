When AI Meets Expensive Capital: Can the Investment Boom Survive Higher Yields?

The AI boom has entered a more demanding phase. Investors no longer ask only whether models are improving; they also ask whether the economics can endure when money is expensive. Ahead of Nvidia’s latest results, markets are weighing strong AI revenue against rising bond yields and the cost of vast data-center programs. That shift matters because a technology can be transformative while the companies financing it still face a fragile capital structure.

The first mechanism is the cost of capital. A company may finance a new hyperscaler campus（超大规模数据中心园区）with cash, bonds, leases or project debt. When interest rates rise, the funding cost increases and the present value of distant profits falls. The effect is larger when a firm relies on leverage, because fixed payments must be met even if demand disappoints. Managers therefore need a prudent assessment of how much debt the business can absorb without weakening its liquidity buffer. A cautious board may reallocate capital from marginal projects to those with a clearer path to profitability.

Valuation creates a second channel. AI shares can keep momentum when investors have confidence that future earnings will justify today’s premium, but the market can become volatile when expectations move faster than realized cash flow. A high earnings multiple（市盈率倍数）is not automatically irrational: it may reflect genuine growth. Yet the implication is that small changes in the probability of success can produce a large change in valuation. If the outlook deteriorates, speculation can reverse quickly, and the spread between optimistic and pessimistic estimates may widen.

The financing structure also deserves attention. BIS research notes that a growing part of AI infrastructure spending is debt-financed, while some arrangements involve off-balance-sheet vehicles（表外融资工具）or circular financing（循环融资）. Such structures may diversify sources of finance, but they can also hide where the final exposure sits. If several firms depend on the same customers, chips, power suppliers or private-credit funds, apparent dispersion may conceal common risk. The system may look resilient in normal times yet prove fragile when one shock forces investors to sell specialized assets into a thin market. In an extreme case, a borrower could default, turning a corporate problem into a wider funding shock.

This does not mean an AI contraction is inevitable. Large technology companies still generate substantial revenue, and productivity gains could offset higher financing costs. A successful deployment may reduce operating expenses, improve software output and create a surplus that supports further investment. The important distinction is between technological capability and financial sustainability. Reliability matters because customers will not pay indefinitely for systems that fail to deliver consistent results. A project can be feasible in engineering terms but fail to cross the commercial threshold required to earn an adequate return. That is why benchmark results, customer retention and utilization rates（设备利用率）matter as much as impressive demonstrations.

Macroeconomics adds another layer. If AI investment remains strong, it can support demand even while higher interest rates moderate other sectors. If the boom weakens abruptly, however, the sequence can run in reverse: capital expenditure falls, suppliers lose orders, hiring slows and household wealth may decline as stock prices adjust. In a severe scenario, these effects could amplify a recession; in a milder one, a slowdown would simply remove excess investment and improve long-run efficiency. Central banks must calibrate policy within a coherent framework without assuming either outcome.

A strategic view also has to include the public balance sheet. Public debt complicates the picture because government borrowing competes for global savings. When sovereign yields rise, corporate borrowers may need to offer a larger premium, especially if investors demand compensation for duration risk（久期风险）or inflation uncertainty. A fiscal commitment to sustainable budgets can help contain that pressure, but weak public finances may raise the economy’s overall financing threshold. The interaction is important: an AI company may have a sound product and still face a worse investment environment because the risk-free rate has changed.

There is also a distributional question. The gains from AI may be concentrated in firms that own chips, cloud platforms, data and energy access, while the costs of adjustment are spread across workers, suppliers and communities. Policymakers should acknowledge this imbalance without treating every market correction as a crisis or allowing short-term politics to undermine useful adjustment. A provision that improves disclosure, competition or grid access can strengthen resilience and regulate obvious conflicts of interest, but excessive protection may preserve inefficient projects. The goal is not to guarantee every investment; it is to maintain conditions in which productive firms can finance expansion and weak projects can exit without destabilizing the wider system.

The long-term lesson is that innovation and finance operate on different horizons. Technology may continue to mature even after market enthusiasm cools. The dot-com era is a useful reminder: many valuations collapsed, yet the underlying digital transition continued. For AI, the most credible outlook is therefore neither automatic triumph nor automatic collapse. Investors should evaluate the magnitude of real productivity gains, firms should maintain enough liquidity to survive volatility, and policymakers should monitor whether debt-funded expansion creates risks that private balance sheets cannot contain.
