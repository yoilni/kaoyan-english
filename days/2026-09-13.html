The Price of the Stream: Why Sports Piracy Is an Economic Problem

Sports piracy is no longer a side issue for football economics. On September 11, Canal+ and LaLiga announced an anti-piracy agreement covering nearly 50 countries. Reuters reported that Spanish clubs’ audiovisual income fell 5.2% to €1.43 billion in 2024/25, while commercial revenue rose to €1.58 billion. That contrast puts the broadcaster’s problem under sharper scrutiny: illegal viewing can weaken a rights market even while the wider sports business keeps growing.

The basic infringement is simple but the delivery chain is not. An illicit service may capture a live signal, hide its origin behind layers of hosting, and send a retransmission through a transnational syndicate. Some networks use counterfeit apps or stolen credential databases, while others circumvent geoblocking or authentication controls. For a legitimate operator, the challenge is therefore not only to find one pirate stream, but to understand a constantly changing technical and commercial network.

Technology can raise deterrence, but no single tool is decisive. A broadcaster can embed a forensic watermark or digital fingerprint in a stream, strengthen encryption, and issue a rapid takedown request when a leak appears. Yet a sophisticated aggregator can shift domains, alter delivery routes, or move servers across territorial borders. The trajectory of enforcement becomes a contest between detection speed and the pirate network’s ability to reappear.

Law adds another layer. Rights holders may seek an injunction, begin litigation, or ask authorities to confiscate equipment linked to organized piracy. A prosecution can impose punitive consequences, and successful claimants may seek damages from a defendant. But statutory rules differ across countries, and a precedent from one judiciary may carry little weight before another tribunal. Even where the underlying copyright is clear, enforcement may not have cross-border parity.

This is why the Canal+–LaLiga pact matters beyond one league. Joint investigations can pool proprietary data, technical expertise and local contacts. They can also help partners consult regulators about whether existing rules are enforceable against fast-moving networks. In some markets, legislative reform may be needed to clarify which intermediaries must respond, how quickly courts should adjudicate urgent cases, and when extraterritorial cooperation is appropriate. Where private disputes cannot be settled directly, arbitration can sometimes reduce delay before formal court proceedings.

However, stronger rules create trade-offs. A coercive blocking regime can reduce leakage, but overly broad orders may affect lawful services or place an onerous burden on smaller intermediaries. Stringent regulation therefore requires procedural safeguards, evidence standards and meaningful scrutiny. The goal is not maximum blocking at any cost; it is a system in which enforcement is targeted, reviewable and proportionate.

The economics are equally important. Sports rights are often sold with territorial exclusivity because a broadcaster expects to recoup large upfront payments through subscriptions and advertising. If piracy expands, a paying subscriber may cancel, increasing churn, or choose an illegal bundle that combines many channels at a tiny price. That can cannibalize legal demand and reduce the remuneration available to clubs, leagues and production partners.

Yet lost views do not translate one-for-one into lost revenue. Some users of piracy would never have paid the market price, so headline estimates can exaggerate the distributional effect. Others may switch when legal products become easier to buy, more reliable or better bundled. A prudential analysis therefore separates consumer access problems from deliberate commercial piracy and avoids treating every illegal stream as economically identical.

There is also a competition question. Anti-piracy enforcement can protect investment, but exclusivity and aggressive blocking can reinforce incumbents if access to content becomes too concentrated. Regulatory authorities must therefore distinguish protection of rights from protection of market power. A sensible backstop is transparent oversight: platforms, leagues and broadcasters should explain their methods, offer a route for appeal, and provide enough evidence for supervisory review without exposing the very tools pirates could copy. A digital publisher using match clips or a player’s likeness may also need a clear licence, because anti-piracy rules should not erase distinctions among commentary, promotion and unlawful substitution.

The long-term lesson is institutional. Piracy will not disappear because one technology, one court ruling or one agreement is powerful enough. Durable enforcement depends on coordination among rights holders, courts, hosting providers and public authority. It also depends on authenticity and trust: users need confidence that legal services are safe and that enforcement respects consent and due process. As the jurisprudence develops, the most credible model will combine fast action with enforceable limits rather than treating stronger control as an automatic substitute for better products.
