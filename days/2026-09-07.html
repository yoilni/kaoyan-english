AI Safety Across Borders: Why Global Rules Need More Than Shared Fear

Artificial intelligence is becoming a test of international cooperation as much as technical capability. Reuters reported on September 7 that U.N. human rights chief Volker Türk warned that advanced AI could pose an existential risk and called for stronger safety guarantees. The warning matters because no single country can govern a technology whose models, chips, data and users cross borders. A serious dialogue therefore has to connect technical risk with political responsibility.

The first difficulty is that countries do not begin from the same position. A bilateral treaty can be detailed, while multilateral diplomacy must accommodate many interests at once. Governments may agree on a general norm but disagree on the standard used to measure compliance. This is why efforts to harmonize rules often move slowly even when there is broad consensus that some common framework is needed.

AI governance also raises questions of sovereignty and jurisdiction. A regulatory authority and its regulator can control firms operating inside its territory, but a model may be trained in one country, hosted in another and used globally. Clear reporting can improve transparency, yet excessive disclosure may expose commercial secrets or security weaknesses. The challenge is to create rules that preserve legitimacy without pretending that national law can perfectly control a transnational system.

One useful goal is alignment between technical safety and public policy. Developers need enough autonomy to improve models, but autonomy is not immunity from responsibility. Rules should focus on foreseeable misuse and abuse rather than assume that every powerful system is automatically harmful. A proportionate precaution can reduce danger without freezing useful research.

Human rights add another layer. AI can affect privacy, consent, liberty and dignity when systems make decisions about people. It can also reproduce discrimination if training data reflect unequal treatment. The policy objective should therefore include equality and inclusion, not only technical performance. If a system causes harm, people need a practical remedy and a route for redress rather than only a general promise that future models will improve.

Enforcement is where many international principles become difficult. A rule without a penalty may be ignored, but a severe sanction can encourage companies or governments to hide problems. Certification and licensing may help in high-risk uses, while a public registry can make important systems easier to identify. These tools work best when supervision is based on evidence instead of paperwork alone.

Verification is especially important when governments distrust one another. A claim that a model is safe should not depend only on self-reporting. Independent monitoring, technical tests and traceability can make it easier to check whether commitments are real. Provenance—knowing where data, models or generated material came from—can also improve attribution when harmful activity occurs.

The United States and China are preparing for a mid-September AI safety dialogue, Reuters reported on September 4. Even limited engagement could be useful because major powers share an interest in preventing uncontrolled cyber incidents. Reciprocity matters: each side is more likely to share information if it expects similar behavior from the other. But negotiation becomes harder when a safety dispute is mixed with trade, chips or broader geopolitical rivalry.

This is where mediation and careful diplomacy can reduce escalation. A technical incident should not automatically trigger retaliation before facts are verified. Predictability matters because governments make safer decisions when they understand the likely response to an event. Shared procedures can also improve interoperability between national warning systems even when the countries do not agree on every political issue.

Corporate governance matters too. A shareholder may prefer rapid product release, while a regulator may emphasize caution and liability. Companies therefore need internal processes that integrate ethics into engineering decisions. Ethical review should not be a decorative committee; it should influence testing, release conditions and the allocation of responsibility when a system fails.

The financial system offers a useful analogy. Recent bank recapitalization debates show that a borrower, lender and supervisor respond to different incentives and why resilience is not created by one rule alone: institutions need buffers, supervision and credible recovery plans. AI governance is similar. A safety framework should preserve the capability to respond when prevention fails, rather than assume perfect control is possible. A prototype rule can be tested, measured and revised before it becomes universal.

International institutions also face a representation problem. Countries that host the largest AI firms have more direct influence, but smaller economies are affected by the same systems. Meaningful participation can improve legitimacy because rules are more durable when those subject to them have a voice. This does not require every state to have identical power, but it does require procedures that take different risks and levels of development seriously.

The hardest problem may be enforcement across borders. An obligation accepted in one jurisdiction can be difficult to apply elsewhere, and a company can restructure its portfolio of services faster than governments can negotiate. That is why cooperation needs both legal agreements and technical mechanisms. Reliability should be demonstrated through repeated tests, not inferred from a single successful demonstration.

The broader lesson is that global AI governance will probably develop through layers rather than one grand treaty. Bilateral dialogue can address urgent risks, multilateral institutions can build common principles, and national governments can enforce local rules. Progress will depend on transparency, verification and a willingness to revise standards as technology changes. The aim is not perfect uniformity, but enough cooperation to prevent dangerous gaps while preserving useful innovation.
