AI Infrastructure: When Intelligence Becomes a Power-System Problem

The AI boom is often described as a race for better algorithms and faster chips. Yet its physical foundation is becoming equally important. On August 28, Reuters reported that Australian data-centre operator NextDC recorded higher water and energy use even as revenue rose. The figures reveal a broader tension: digital intelligence may appear weightless to users, but producing it requires buildings, cooling equipment, electricity networks and water. As AI expands, the industry must accommodate rapidly growing computation while limiting the environmental consequence of that growth.

The scale is already significant. The International Energy Agency says data centres accounted for about 1.5% of global electricity demand in 2025 and projects their electricity consumption to roughly double by 2030. AI-focused facilities can demand far more power than conventional data centres. This does not mean AI will overwhelm the entire global grid; the IEA's base case still puts data centres at just under 3% of worldwide electricity use in 2030. But the global percentage can conceal severe local pressure. A large facility connected to one regional network can constrain available capacity even when its global share looks small.

This difference between global and local effects is critical. Electricity cannot always be moved cheaply from wherever it is abundant to wherever a server cluster is built. Transmission lines, substations（变电站） and transformers have geographic limits. Developers therefore increasingly navigate a market in which access to power can matter as much as access to land. Reuters reported on August 19 that European AI data-centre developers are exploring less traditional locations because cheaper energy, cheaper land and faster grid connections can improve project economics.

That shift can alter regional development. A town with abundant electricity and suitable land may attract investment that once concentrated near major cities. Construction can stimulate local demand and expand the tax base, while new transmission infrastructure may also benefit other users. But the benefits are not automatically uniform. Data centres are capital-intensive（资本密集型） and may create fewer permanent jobs than their investment totals suggest. Communities can therefore question whether public infrastructure should be expanded primarily for private computing facilities.

The problem is partly one of timing. A data centre may become operational within a few years, whereas power plants and transmission projects often require longer planning and construction periods. This mismatch can create a bottleneck. If grid expansion lags behind computing investment, firms may seek temporary generation or locate elsewhere. The IEA notes that transformers, turbines and grid connections have become important physical constraints. In other words, AI's execution problem increasingly extends beyond semiconductor factories into the energy system.

Efficiency offers one response. Engineers can refine chips, cooling systems and software so that each AI task uses less electricity. Better utilization can also raise the amount of useful computation produced by existing infrastructure. Yet efficiency creates a familiar economic complication known as the rebound effect（反弹效应）: when a service becomes cheaper to provide, people may use much more of it. Falling energy use per AI query does not necessarily ensure falling total consumption if the number and complexity of queries grow faster.

This is why demand forecasts contain substantial uncertainty. Future electricity use depends on model efficiency, adoption rates, hardware design and the kinds of AI applications that become common. It would therefore be premature to treat any single projection as certain. A sensible infrastructure strategy must incorporate several scenarios rather than assume one fixed path. Utilities need enough capacity to avoid shortages, but excessive construction can leave consumers paying for assets that later prove unnecessary.

Water adds another dimension. Data centres may use water directly for cooling or indirectly through electricity generation. NextDC's latest results showed rising water-use and power-use effectiveness metrics, drawing attention to resource intensity as capacity expands. The appropriate policy response depends on location: water consumption may be manageable in one region but controversial in another facing scarcity. Regulators therefore need to assess local conditions rather than impose a single global rule.

Energy sourcing is similarly complex. Technology companies often commit to renewable electricity, but a contractual purchase does not always mean that a data centre receives carbon-free power every hour. Solar and wind output varies, while computing demand is relatively continuous. Storage, transmission, natural gas and nuclear generation can therefore complement renewables. The IEA expects renewables to meet nearly half of additional data-centre electricity demand over the next five years, with other sources also playing important roles.

The investment response is spreading through the economy. Reuters reported this week that power supplier Aggreko filed for a U.S. IPO amid strong data-centre demand, while nVent agreed to acquire data-centre equipment maker Maverick Power for $1.75 billion. Such transactions illustrate how an AI boom can extend beyond chipmakers: electrical equipment, cooling, construction and power generation can all become part of the AI supply chain. Investors must nevertheless distinguish structural demand from temporary enthusiasm, because infrastructure assets can remain in place long after technology cycles change.

Policy makers face a difficult allocation problem. They want to encourage innovation without allowing large new loads to displace households or conventional industry from affordable electricity. One approach is to make developers bear more of the cost of grid upgrades they require. Another is to accelerate permitting for generation and transmission that benefits the wider system. Neither approach is costless: strict requirements can delay investment, while weak requirements can transfer costs to other electricity users.

There is also a strategic dimension. Countries increasingly view AI infrastructure as part of national technological capability. Reliable electricity can support domestic computing capacity, but attempting to build every component locally may be inefficient. Governments must decide which dependencies are acceptable and which create excessive vulnerability. This calculation resembles semiconductor policy: resilience may justify some additional cost, but complete self-sufficiency is rarely realistic.

For companies, the central lesson is that compute is no longer merely an IT expense. Power availability, grid queues（电网接入排队）, water conditions and construction schedules can determine whether a planned AI service can actually scale. Firms that ignore these constraints may discover that financial capital cannot instantly create physical capacity. Conversely, infrastructure companies that assume demand will rise forever may overestimate the durability of today's boom.

The long-run outcome will depend on a balance between expansion and efficiency. More efficient chips may moderate energy growth per unit of computation, while wider AI adoption pushes total demand upward. Grid investment can relieve bottlenecks, but it takes time. Renewable generation can reduce emissions, yet reliability requires a broader system. These forces are not mutually exclusive; they interact.

The deeper point is economic rather than technological. Every digital revolution eventually encounters physical constraints. The cloud is built on land; software runs on machines; intelligence consumes energy. AI may continue to improve rapidly, but its growth will increasingly depend on society's ability to build infrastructure, price scarce resources and coordinate investment across industries. The next phase of the AI race may therefore be won not only by whoever designs the smartest model, but by whoever can connect computation to the physical economy most effectively.
