The Business of Being Relatable: Why Ordinary Content Can Be Valuable

Social media is turning the mundane parts of work into a commercial product. Reuters Breakingviews noted on September 4 that day-at-work videos now attract viewers with scenes as simple as commuting, typing and eating lunch. The appeal lies partly in routine: familiar activities are easy to understand, while a relatable creator can create a sense of intimacy without producing expensive entertainment. This is a useful example of how the creator ecosystem can convert ordinary life into media inventory.

The business model depends on more than popularity. A creator develops a public persona, while a brand pays for an endorsement because it hopes some of the creator’s credibility will transfer to a product. That makes disclosure important: audiences should know when persuasion is commercial. IAB projects U.S. creator advertising spending at about $43.9 billion in 2026, which helps explain why compliance and measurement are becoming more important as the market grows.

There is a tension between aspiration and familiarity. Traditional advertising often presents an idealized lifestyle, but smaller creators can appear more spontaneous and less scripted. That difference can matter when audiences are tired of polished promotions. Yet success creates its own problem: once the same format spreads everywhere, saturation reduces novelty. A narrow niche can preserve distinctiveness, but it also limits scale.

Brands therefore need to separate engagement from business results. Likes and comments may indicate interest, but they do not automatically produce conversion. Attribution is difficult because a consumer may see several videos before making a purchase. An affiliate link can make tracking easier, while a commission gives creators a direct financial incentive, but both can encourage aggressive selling if poorly designed.

For platforms, visibility is a scarce resource. Human editors perform curation, while automated systems decide which posts receive prominent placement. A paid promotion can expand reach, but a campaign that depends entirely on purchased distribution may disappear when spending stops. This makes platform design part of the economics of creator work, not simply a technical background condition.

A marketer also has to interpret noisy signals. An impression only shows that content was displayed; it does not prove that the viewer trusted or even noticed it. An influencer with millions of followers may generate broad awareness, while a smaller but genuine voice may have a stronger relationship with a specific group. In that sense, ordinary content can outperform spectacle when the commercial goal depends on credibility rather than pure reach.

Production style contributes to that effect. Informal videos can feel closer to conversation than to advertising, and creators can personalize examples for particular communities. Small details matter: a subtle product mention may feel natural, while an obvious sales pitch can interrupt the story. Repetition can build recognition over time, but too much repetition can make the audience feel manipulated.

The psychology is partly emotional. People often remember information that is connected to emotion or a familiar personal story. Through repeated association, a creator can make a product category feel less abstract. Familiarity may lower the mental cost of evaluating a message, while reciprocity can appear when followers feel that a creator has repeatedly provided useful information. That relationship has value only when the creator remains trustworthy.

Creators also learn through feedback. Comments, watch time and purchasing data reveal patterns in audience behavior, giving creators a reason to revise what they make. Financial motivation can improve consistency, but it can also narrow creativity if every idea is judged only by immediate performance. A healthy creator business therefore needs both experimentation and discipline, much like any other small enterprise.

The same system can become intrusive. Constant sponsored posts create distraction, while information overload makes it harder for any message to stand out. Media fragmentation adds another challenge because audiences are divided across many platforms and formats. For a small creator, discoverability can therefore be more difficult even when the total market is growing. The market may expand while individual creators still struggle to be seen.

Commercial growth also raises questions of fairness and deception. If advertising resembles personal advice too closely, viewers may misunderstand the creator’s financial interest. Clear disclosure cannot eliminate every conflict, but it gives audiences information needed to judge the message. Platforms and advertisers have an accountability problem as well: they benefit from creator advertising, so responsibility should not fall only on the individual creator.

Artificial intelligence may increase production efficiency by helping creators edit, translate and test content. It may also increase saturation by making cheap content easier to produce. The economic question is not whether AI can generate more posts, but whether additional output creates more useful attention. Innovation that lowers production costs is valuable only if it improves the match between creators, audiences and advertisers rather than simply increasing noise.

Investors should therefore be cautious about simple growth stories. A creator company can show strong traction while still depending on one platform or one advertising category. The narrative may sound attractive, but an investor should ask how stable the revenue is, whether contracts are durable and whether compliance costs rise with scale. Diligence matters because creator businesses can grow quickly without becoming equally predictable.

Smaller creators also reveal why market power is not only about size. They may have greater agency over tone and community relationships, while brands can launch a limited initiative before spending heavily. The objective should be to demonstrate whether a partnership changes behavior, not merely whether it creates views. Good experiments produce evidence that can guide later investment and reduce the risk of copying a trend simply because competitors are doing it.

The long-term lesson is that creator advertising will probably become more professional without becoming identical to traditional media. Its efficiency comes from combining low-cost production with social relationships, but every externality matters: excessive promotion can reduce trust across the whole market. Brands must decide which outcomes deserve priority, and audiences will continue to shape consumption through what they ignore as much as through what they watch. Sustainable growth requires creativity, measurement and accountability at the same time.
