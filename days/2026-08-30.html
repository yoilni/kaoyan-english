Inflation Expectations: Why Markets Move Before Central Banks Do

Central banks change interest rates only at scheduled meetings, but financial markets move every day. This gap matters because investors do not wait for a formal decision: they constantly estimate what policymakers are likely to do next. At Jackson Hole on August 28, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh stressed that the Fed's 2% inflation objective remains firm and that policymakers must be confident underlying inflation is moving toward it at sufficient speed. The immediate market reaction showed a basic principle of modern finance: expectations can change borrowing costs before the central bank itself changes the policy rate.

To understand the mechanism, consider a ten-year government bond. Its yield reflects not only today's short-term rate but also expectations about future rates, future inflation and risk. If traders believe inflation will remain high, they may demand greater compensation for holding a fixed-income asset whose purchasing power could erode. This can widen long-term yields even when the central bank leaves its current rate unchanged. In that sense, monetary policy operates partly through beliefs.

Warsh argued that inflation expectations remain broadly anchored, but he also warned that such confidence can appear durable until it suddenly does not. The distinction is critical. If households and firms believe price growth will stay high, workers may demand a higher wage, businesses may raise prices in advance, and lenders may require a larger margin against inflation risk. Expectations can then become partly self-reinforcing rather than a passive forecast.

This does not mean every rise in expected inflation will automatically produce actual inflation. Economic behavior is constrained by competition, productivity and household income. A company may want to raise prices but be unable to do so if customers can easily switch to a rival. Likewise, employees may seek higher pay but have limited bargaining power when unemployment is rising. The strength of the expectations channel therefore depends on the wider economic environment.

The recent U.S. data illustrate the difficulty of interpretation. Reuters reported that July PCE inflation rose faster than economists had expected, while August consumer confidence weakened and households reported higher expected inflation. Policymakers must assess whether these signals reveal a persistent trend or temporary noise. Acting too slowly can allow inflation pressure to prevail; acting too aggressively can depress investment and employment.

This is why central banking is not simply a contest between inflation and growth. The more difficult task is to decide which risk deserves greater weight at a particular moment. If inflation remains above target while the labor market is still stable, a central bank may incline toward tighter policy. If employment suddenly weakens, the same institution may become more neutral or even supportive. The appropriate choice depends on the balance of evidence, not on one indicator.

Communication is part of that balance. A central bank tries to be accountable to the public while avoiding promises that future data may make impossible to keep. Too much certainty can mislead investors; too little guidance can increase volatility. Warsh has emphasized discipline rather than a fixed decision, which places more responsibility on markets to interpret data. That approach may improve flexibility, but it may also make each inflation report or employment release a stronger market factor.

Credibility is therefore an economic asset. When investors believe the central bank will eventually restore price stability, long-term inflation expectations may stay contained even during a temporary shock. But credibility cannot simply be declared. Policymakers must repeatedly show consistency between their stated goals and their actions. A failure to follow through can impair confidence and raise the cost of stabilizing prices later.

There is also a fiscal dimension. Monetary policy does not operate in isolation from government borrowing. When public debt is large and investors demand higher yields, interest expenses can rise quickly. Governments then face a greater obligation to finance debt service, which can crowd out other spending or require higher taxes. If markets believe fiscal policy is becoming unsustainable, the central bank may face additional pressure even when its own mandate has not changed.

This creates a dispute over how monetary and fiscal authorities should interact. Should a central bank ever tolerate slightly higher inflation to reduce the burden of government debt? In principle, doing so would contradict a firm price-stability mandate. It could also interfere with central-bank independence by making monetary policy subordinate to fiscal needs. Yet excessively tight policy can also worsen debt dynamics by slowing growth. The correct response is not to ignore fiscal conditions, but to preserve a clear division of responsibility.

International spillovers make the problem harder. A higher U.S. interest-rate path can strengthen the dollar, increase funding costs abroad and trigger capital movement across borders. Countries with large dollar liabilities may have to defer investment or tighten their own policies. A weak domestic currency can raise import prices and contribute to inflation. Thus a decision made to stabilize one economy can create pressure elsewhere.

Japan provides an example of this global interaction. Recent Reuters reporting shows that Japanese policymakers are also confronting inflation risks while the yen remains weak. If the Bank of Japan tightens policy while the Fed also stays restrictive, exchange rates may move differently than investors previously expected. Cross-border portfolios then need a new allocation, and market participants may scrutinize central-bank language for clues about relative policy paths.

One reason this process is difficult is that economic models are imperfect. Policymakers cannot directly observe the economy's exact equilibrium in real time. They must infer it from imperfect data and revise their views as new information arrives. A model that looked reliable in one decade may become less useful after a technological or geopolitical change. This is why Warsh emphasized the need for more contemporary and actionable information rather than blind dependence on old relationships.

The rise of artificial intelligence adds another uncertainty. AI could raise productivity and make some goods cheaper, but heavy investment in data centers can also create enormous demand for electricity, construction and specialized labor. These effects may occur at different speeds. A central bank should not exaggerate either the inflationary or disinflationary impact before evidence is available. It must instead monitor whether productivity gains become broad enough to change the economy's equilibrium.

For investors, the lesson is that a policy speech should not be interpreted as a direct trading instruction. Markets often react strongly because participants are updating probabilities, not because a rate move has already happened. A single speech can change the expected pace of policy, but upcoming employment, inflation and spending data can reverse that interpretation. The sensible approach is to examine the full distribution of risks rather than treat one statement as a guarantee.

The same principle applies to households. A homeowner deciding whether to borrow, or a business deciding whether to build a new factory, faces uncertainty about future financing costs. They may restrain spending if rates are expected to rise, even before actual rates move. This is one reason expectations have real economic consequences: they influence present decisions through anticipated future conditions.

However, expectations can also be wrong. Markets sometimes overestimate the probability of a recession or underestimate the persistence of inflation. When new information arrives, prices can reverse sharply. A healthy financial system must therefore be able to absorb changing beliefs without excessive instability. Market reliability depends not on perfect forecasts but on sufficient liquidity, transparent pricing and institutions that can survive error.

Distributional effects should not be ignored. Higher interest rates may help savers but hurt borrowers, especially younger households with mortgages or firms that depend on credit. Inflation also creates inequality because households do not consume the same goods or hold the same assets. A policy that appears desirable in aggregate can impose very different costs across groups. This is why monetary policy inevitably involves trade-offs even when its formal goals are clear.

Some critics argue that central banks should communicate less because constant commentary creates noise. Others believe more explanation strengthens public trust. A reasonable consensus is difficult because transparency has both benefits and costs. More communication can clarify the institution's reaction function（政策反应函数）, but frequent speeches can also generate conflicting signals. The challenge is to explain principles without pretending that uncertain future decisions are already settled.

Institutional design also matters. Central banks are usually given operational independence because elected governments may have a short-term motive to keep borrowing costs low. Independence allows policymakers to pursue price stability even when the decision is unpopular. But independence does not remove democratic accountability. Legislatures can define the mandate, review performance and authorize the institution's powers without dictating each rate decision.

The effectiveness of policy also depends on execution. A credible framework is of little value if data systems fail, market operations are poorly coordinated or officials develop excessive dependency on a single model. Institutional initiative should therefore focus on better measurement, scenario analysis and operational resilience rather than on producing ever more confident forecasts.

The long-run goal is not permanently high interest rates. It is a monetary system in which inflation is sufficiently stable that people do not need to think about it constantly. When credibility is strong, the central bank may need less aggressive intervention because expectations themselves help stabilize behavior. When credibility weakens, restoring it can require more painful adjustment. That is why policymakers often devote so much attention to expectations that seem abstract to ordinary consumers.

Ultimately, monetary policy is an exercise in managing uncertainty rather than eliminating it. Policymakers must conform to a clear mandate while remaining flexible about the path used to achieve it. They must compensate for imperfect data, devote attention to both inflation and employment, and avoid becoming excessively conservative when new evidence requires change. They also need to distinguish a genuine exception from a lasting trend.

There is also a practical question of cost. A tight policy stance can raise the price of credit throughout the economy. A firm may cancel an economical expansion project because financing has become too expensive, while a public investment fund may reduce new commitments. If such restraint is gradual, the economy may adjust smoothly; if it is abrupt, a slight change in expectations can produce a much larger response in asset prices.

Policy debate should therefore not neglect institutional reform. Better data, clearer mandates and more steady communication can reduce avoidable uncertainty. A well-designed reform does not guarantee perfect decisions, but it can make mistakes easier to detect and correct. It can also reduce the risk that one dominant indicator—such as a single inflation release—receives too much attention.

The most scarce resource in this process may be credibility itself. It takes years to build and can weaken quickly if policy appears inconsistent. A successful central bank does not suppress every price movement or attempt to control every financial variable. Instead, it tries to keep the broader nominal environment stable enough for households and firms to make long-term decisions.

That perspective also explains why policy can feel paradoxical. Tightening today may be intended to protect future prosperity, even if it slows activity in the short run. A temporary slowdown can be preferable to allowing inflation to become entrenched（根深蒂固）. Yet if policymakers tighten beyond what is necessary, they may produce a stagnant economy and unnecessary unemployment. The challenge is not to choose between pain and no pain, but to minimize the total cost over time.

Policy can also be reversed. If inflation falls convincingly and demand weakens, the central bank may begin a withdrawal from restrictive policy by reducing rates. Such a move is not necessarily an admission that previous tightening was wrong; it may simply mean the balance of risks has changed. Good policy must remain flexible enough to respond when evidence changes.

For markets, then, the central question after Jackson Hole is not simply whether the Fed will raise rates at its next meeting. It is whether incoming data strengthen or weaken the case that underlying inflation is moving toward 2% at a sufficient speed. Investors will signal their answer through bond yields, currencies and equity prices long before policymakers vote. That is why expectations matter: the future begins affecting the economy before it arrives.
