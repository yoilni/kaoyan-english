AI and Financial Stability: When One Digital Failure Becomes a Systemic Risk

Artificial intelligence is changing finance in two opposite ways. It can make fraud detection faster, improve risk analysis and automate routine decisions, yet the same technology may also amplify a cyberattack by lowering the cost of finding weaknesses and generating convincing deception. On August 31, the Financial Stability Board (FSB) warned G20 finance ministers and central-bank governors that frontier AI may materially alter the speed, scale and economics of cyber risk. The issue is therefore no longer only whether one bank can stop one hacker; it is whether a digital shock can spread through an interconnected financial system.

Financial stability depends heavily on trust. A depositor does not inspect a bank's servers before making a payment, and an investor usually assumes an exchange will process a transaction correctly. This quiet assurance allows millions of decisions to occur without constant verification. A serious data breach, however, can suddenly make people question whether records are accurate, identities are protected and payments are valid. Once trust weakens, the economic damage may exceed the direct technical loss.

The key concept is systemic risk. A local failure becomes systemic when its effects cascade through institutions that depend on one another. Suppose a cloud provider suffers a major disruption and several banks simultaneously lose access to a critical service. Customers may be unable to transfer money, firms may delay payroll, and markets may struggle to settle trades. Even if no bank is insolvent, operational failure can create behavior that resembles a financial crisis.

AI may increase this danger because digital finance often relies on a high degree of concentration. Many firms purchase computing, cybersecurity or model services from a small number of technology companies. Such centralized infrastructure is efficient when everything works, but it can create a shared vulnerability. A single technical weakness may then affect many institutions at once rather than remain isolated inside one company.

This creates a difficult trade-off. Standardized systems can improve compatibility and lower costs, yet excessive dependence on the same provider can reduce redundancy（冗余备份）. In engineering, redundancy means having another path when the first fails. Finance needs the same principle: backup communication channels, alternative data centers and recovery plans that can withstand stress. Such preparation may appear inefficient during normal periods, but its value becomes obvious during an emergency.

The FSB's concern also reflects the changing economics of attack. A malicious actor can use AI to search code, imitate communication styles or generate many variants of a scam. This does not mean AI automatically defeats every defense. Rather, it can increase the number of attempts and make some attacks more persuasive. A bank may block 99.9% of suspicious activity and still face a problem if the remaining 0.1% is large enough or targets a crucial institution.

For financial firms, the first response is strong governance. Senior managers cannot treat AI risk as an issue for the IT department alone. They need clear responsibility for model use, third-party services and incident response. Boards should remain accountable for major technology decisions, while regulators should provide proportionate oversight rather than assume that every institution faces identical risks.

Technical controls matter as well. Firms need a clear security protocol for identifying users, limiting access and recording unusual activity. Sensitive information should remain confidential, and unauthorized access should trigger investigation. AI can assist by detecting an anomaly（异常模式） in network behavior, but automated alerts must still be interpreted carefully. A strange pattern can indicate an attack, a software error or simply unusual customer activity.

This is where human judgment remains important. An institution may use AI to trace the origin of suspicious behavior or support forensic analysis（取证分析） after an incident. Yet a model can also make mistakes, especially when it faces data unlike its training examples. Security teams should therefore verify critical outputs rather than accept them automatically. The aim is not to reject automation but to combine machine speed with human responsibility.

Another risk is fraud. Generative systems can produce realistic messages, documents and voices, which may help criminals impersonate customers or executives. Banks therefore need stronger methods to authenticate identity（身份认证） and to detect when a request conflicts with normal behavior. But adding too many checks can also interrupt legitimate payments and frustrate customers. Security has to be strong enough to reduce risk without making ordinary commerce unnecessarily difficult.

Institutions also need to examine their partners. A bank may be secure internally while a smaller intermediary—such as a software vendor or payment processor—has weaker defenses. An attacker can then exploit the weakest link in a supply chain. Effective supervision must therefore extend beyond the largest banks. Regulators should supervise important dependencies and ask whether an insurer, asset manager or payment firm could transmit stress to others.

This possibility resembles financial contagion. In a traditional banking panic, fear spreads because depositors worry that one institution's losses may reveal similar problems elsewhere. In a cyber event, institutions may share software, data services or authentication systems, so the same weakness can appear in multiple places. The result can be simultaneous disruption, which is harder to manage than several unrelated failures occurring at different times.

Authorities therefore need cross-border cooperation. Digital services do not stop at a national border, while legal powers still operate within a jurisdiction. A cloud provider can serve banks in dozens of countries, but each regulator may have different reporting rules. Without mutual recognition and information sharing, firms can face conflicting demands during a fast-moving incident. The FSB argues that global coordination is important because fragmented rules may leave gaps precisely where risks cross borders.

Yet regulation also creates costs. If every new AI system requires lengthy approval, innovation may slow and smaller firms may be unable to compete. If rules are too weak, however, companies may overlook risks whose costs will eventually be borne by the wider system. A robust framework should therefore focus on outcomes: institutions should demonstrate that they can identify threats, protect critical services and recover when controls fail, rather than merely complete paperwork.

Recovery is especially important because perfect prevention is impossible. A resilient system assumes that some defenses will fail. It should be able to suspend compromised functions, isolate affected networks and restore essential services quickly. This requires a decisive response, but speed alone is not enough. Acting on incorrect information can worsen the incident, so emergency plans must balance rapid action with careful confirmation.

The financial effect of a cyberattack can also spread through markets. If investors believe a major institution has lost control of its systems, its reputation may deteriorate before the true scale of the damage is known. Counterparties may demand more collateral（抵押品）, customers may withdraw funds, and market makers may reduce activity. The resulting pressure on liquidity shows why cyber risk cannot be separated neatly from traditional financial risk.

There is also a macroeconomic dimension. If many banks become cautious after a cyber incident, they may tighten lending even when their balance sheets remain healthy. Businesses could postpone investment, households might delay purchases, and confidence could weaken. A technology shock can therefore associate operational risk with broader economic behavior, although the strength of this link depends on the duration and severity of the disruption.

The FSB's June consultation on responsible AI adoption proposed 12 sound practices covering organization-wide governance, AI development, deployment and third-party risk. This approach reflects an inherent difficulty: supervisors need rules that remain useful even as models evolve quickly. A highly detailed rule may become obsolete after several consecutive generations of technology, while a rule that is too general may provide little practical guidance.

One solution is to focus on capabilities rather than brand names or individual models. Institutions should be able to explain what a system can do, what data it uses, where its deficiency lies and how failure could affect customers. They should also define a safeguard for high-impact decisions and test whether controls still work when conditions change. This makes regulation more adaptable without abandoning responsibility.

The challenge becomes harder when AI itself is used in defense. If both attackers and defenders improve simultaneously, security becomes an evolving contest rather than a problem with a final solution. A new protective tool may reduce one hazard while creating another—for example, by giving too much authority to an automated system. Institutions need constant vigilance, not because disaster is inevitable, but because risk changes as technology changes.

Some observers fear that powerful AI will inevitably destabilize finance. That conclusion is too strong. Technology can also strengthen defenses, improve detection and accelerate incident response. The more useful question is whether institutions can adopt AI faster than they can build the controls needed to manage it. If adoption outruns governance, the gap itself becomes a source of risk.

This is why resilience remains a better goal than perfect security. A resilient financial system can absorb mistakes, continue critical functions and recover without creating panic. It does not promise that every threat will be prevented. Instead, it limits the damage when prevention fails. That principle is familiar in banking regulation, where capital and liquidity buffers exist because losses cannot be eliminated entirely.

AI also raises questions about responsibility. If an automated system recommends a harmful action, who should bear the ultimate responsibility: the bank, the software provider, the model developer or the employee who approved the decision? A clear legal answer is necessary because uncertainty can encourage every participant to shift blame. Rules should prevent firms from using automation to compromise existing duties to customers.

At the same time, regulators should avoid assuming that every AI error is a regulatory violation. Innovation involves experimentation, and some failures are unavoidable. The important distinction is whether an institution acted responsibly: Did it understand the risk, test the system, protect sensitive data and respond appropriately when a problem appeared? That standard is stricter than simple optimism but more flexible than prohibiting new technology.

The broader lesson is that modern finance is increasingly an information system as well as a money system. Banks, exchanges and payment networks depend on data being accurate, available and trusted. AI can improve that system, but it can also increase the speed with which errors or attacks spread. The most dangerous failure may therefore be not the largest initial loss, but the failure that causes people to doubt the reliability of the entire network.

For policymakers, there is no simple consensus about the exact level of regulation. Too much control can suppress useful innovation; too little can expose the public to risks that individual firms have weak incentives to absorb. The legitimate objective is to make private experimentation compatible with public stability. That requires firms to carry an obligation to protect critical services while authorities remain capable of acting when risks cross institutional boundaries.

The long-term trend is therefore likely to be a deeper integration of technology policy and financial supervision. Cybersecurity, model governance and traditional prudential regulation（审慎监管） can no longer be treated as separate fields. As AI becomes embedded in lending, trading and payments, the ability to recover from digital failure will become part of what it means for a financial institution to be safe.

That does not justify panic. It just changes the definition of preparedness. The central question is no longer whether a system can avoid every error, but whether it can absorb one without allowing fear, operational failure and market stress to reinforce each other. In an AI-enabled financial system, the strongest defense may be a combination of technical security, institutional discipline and enough redundancy to keep trust alive when something goes wrong.
