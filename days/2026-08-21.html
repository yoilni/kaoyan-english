AI’s Next Bottleneck: Power, Land and Permission

Recent AI investment is changing the geography （地理分布） of computing. As developers search for sites adjacent to power plants （发电厂） and transmission lines （输电线路）, decisions once driven mainly by fiber networks （光纤网络） and proximity （接近；邻近） to users increasingly depend on electricity affordability. In Europe, new projects are moving farther from major cities, while local governments weigh （权衡） the aggregate benefits against pressure on land, water and the grid （电网）. The key question is no longer whether AI can accelerate; it is how fast physical systems can support it.

The first mechanism （机制） is allocation （资源配置）. A power provider must allocate a limited connection （并网容量） to one project rather than another, and a local authority （地方主管部门） may require public approval before construction begins. That creates a burden for officials who must make companies comply with environmental rules while still attracting investment. A comprehensive assessment （评估） therefore has to compare jobs and tax revenue with long-run electricity consumption. The relevant criterion is not simply how much capital arrives, but whether the project strengthens the wider economy.

This helps explain why developers are exploring a more decentralized map of data centers. Moving to secondary locations （次级地区） may reduce delay in getting a grid connection （并网）, yet it can also displace other industrial users if power is scarce. AI facilities （设施） can dominate local demand because they operate continuously, so efficiency becomes a public concern rather than only a corporate one. Better forecasting （预测） can enable grid operators （电网运营商） to plan upgrades （升级改造）, while regulators （监管机构） may enforce connection standards that prevent one applicant from absorbing （占用） resources without considering others.

Scale changes the economics （经济性）. When demand is expected to exceed existing supply, companies may expand generation （发电能力） or finance new lines, but these choices create an externality if costs are shifted onto residents. The foundation of a fair system is therefore cost causation （成本归因原则）: whoever creates the need for new infrastructure should help generate the revenue required to build it. Governments may impose special conditions, utilities （公用事业企业） may maintain reserve margins （备用容量率）, and planners may minimize disruption （扰动；影响） by asking developers to modify the timing or design of projects. Firms, in turn, need to obtain reliable power without turning public networks into private subsidies （补贴）.

Politics enters because permission is itself scarce. A state can permit construction, but opposition may persist when people fear higher bills, water use or weak transparency （透明度）. Policymakers must decide which objective deserves priority. Some may prohibit fast-track treatment （快速审批待遇）, others may promote projects that bring their own generation, and investors will judge the prospect of future returns against the risk of slower permitting （审批过程）. If a region suffers a power shock, it must recover quickly; rules that restrict peak demand （峰值用电需求） or retain backup supply （备用电力） can improve resilience （韧性；恢复能力） without stopping development entirely.

The financing question is equally important. A huge campus （此处指大型数据中心园区） may look viable when expected AI revenue is high, but any constraint on power delivery （电力输送） can postpone cash flow （现金流）. The incentive to build rapidly is strong because early sites can secure customers, yet a shortage of transformers （变压器）, turbines （涡轮机） or transmission equipment can make schedules unreliable. Recent IEA analysis suggests the investment boom is substantial, while electricity demand from data centers could roughly double by 2030. That does not prove every project has sufficient demand; it means the system must prepare for a range of outcomes.

This is where empirical discipline （实证约束；基于数据判断） matters. Investors need evidence about utilization （利用率）, energy prices and model demand, not only optimistic narratives （乐观叙事）. Public intervention may be justified when transmission is a natural monopoly （自然垄断）, but it should not hide fiscal costs. If grid upgrades are socialized （由社会共同承担成本）, a household that receives little direct benefit may still pay more. The expectation of local employment can also be overstated because construction work is temporary and permanent staffing （长期人员配置） is smaller than the project’s physical size suggests.

The broader challenge is to transform AI growth into durable productivity （生产率）. A data center is a large electricity customer, but the surrounding economy becomes vulnerable if one industry absorbs too much power or if energy prices rise sharply. Good policy should consider welfare as well as corporate returns, allow authorities to withdraw privileges （优惠权利） when promises are not met, and help communities adapt to new demand. Different territory will make different choices: dense urban markets may emphasize conservation （节约资源）, while a regional hub （区域枢纽） with abundant generation may compete aggressively for investment.

The long-term lesson is that compute （算力） is becoming an infrastructure problem as much as a software problem. The most credible strategy is neither automatic expansion nor automatic resistance. It is a negotiated model （协商式模式） that links private capital to grid investment, protects stability, and tests whether each project creates more value than cost.
