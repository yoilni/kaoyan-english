When AI Becomes a Sponsor: The New Trust Economy

For years, the creator economy was built on a simple bargain: platforms supplied distribution（内容分发）, brands supplied money, and creators supplied attention. Generative AI is now altering that bargain. Recent reporting shows that AI companies are paying creators substantial sums to promote new tools, while some audiences react with suspicion. The central issue is therefore not whether creators will use AI—they already do—but how they can preserve trust when the technology they discuss is also the company paying them.

This tension reveals an important economic principle. A creator does not merely sell advertising space; the creator also possesses a stock of reputational capital（声誉资本）. That asset is difficult to measure, yet it can determine future income. A lucrative sponsorship may yield immediate revenue, but repeated promotions that appear inconsistent with a creator’s usual views can undermine credibility. In this sense, audience trust is not a vague moral ideal. It is an economic resource whose value can accumulate slowly and collapse quickly.

The mechanism becomes clearer when we distinguish reach from persuasion. AI can automate editing, research and storyboarding（分镜设计）, allowing one person to produce more material. This may boost output and reduce production costs. Yet a larger volume of content does not necessarily guarantee stronger influence. When audiences suspect that recommendations are manufactured or excessively sponsored, they may resist the message. The creator then faces a familiar economic problem: greater short-term supply can degrade the scarce quality on which long-term demand depends.

Recent deals in the wider media industry point to a second shift. ByteDance and the Motion Picture Association reached an AI copyright pact on August 17, while music companies have increasingly debated whether to license generative-AI systems or pursue litigation. These developments suggest that creative work is becoming not only media for human audiences but also an input asset（投入型资产） for machine systems. Once content has training or grounding（为AI回答提供可靠资料） value, creators and publishers have an incentive to assert ownership, negotiate terms and demand compensation rather than simply accept uncontrolled reuse.

However, licensing is not a complete solution. Large publishers can hire lawyers and negotiate complex contracts, whereas an individual creator may lack bargaining power. If only the biggest rights holders can capture licensing revenue, AI could intensify an existing inequality: small creators produce much of the culture that makes platforms attractive, but intermediaries may capture a disproportionate share of its value. A healthier market would need rules that clarify consent, specify permitted uses and make payments sufficiently accessible to smaller participants.

Transparency matters for the same reason. A sponsored creator should disclose a commercial relationship, but disclosure alone does not automatically restore confidence. Audiences also judge whether the recommendation is consistent with the creator’s previous behavior and whether claims are supported by evidence. Managers may therefore screen potential sponsors more carefully and reject deals whose reputational cost appears too high. This is especially important for AI products because public attitudes remain diverse: some users value productivity gains, while others worry about copyright, employment and synthetic content flooding（大量涌入） online spaces.

Platforms face their own trade-off. Generative tools can lower the cost of participation and help newcomers compete, which may broaden creative opportunity. But if automated accounts produce enormous quantities of repetitive material, discovery systems must distinguish useful work from low-effort output. That creates pressure for provenance（内容来源记录）, watermarking and clearer labels. Such measures may discourage deceptive production without eliminating legitimate AI-assisted creativity.

The labor question is equally complex. AI may substitute for some routine tasks while supplementing human judgment in others. A creator who previously outsourced basic editing may perform it with software, but may simultaneously spend more on research, community management or original reporting. Thus, the effect on employment cannot be inferred from a single task. It depends on whether productivity gains stimulate enough new demand to offset the work that automation removes.

This is where the older vocabulary of platform economics remains useful. Creators still operate under a constraint: they depend on algorithms they do not control. A sudden change in recommendation systems can alter traffic, while a change in monetization rules can affect income. AI adds another layer because platforms may become both distributors and producers of synthetic content. Regulators and creators will therefore need credible methods to monitor whether platforms favor their own generated material or apply rules unevenly.

None of this implies that AI sponsorship or AI-assisted creation is inherently harmful. The more useful distinction is between augmentation and substitution, and between transparent commerce and hidden influence. Creators who adopt useful tools while maintaining editorial independence may strengthen their businesses. Those who exploit audience trust too aggressively may discover that attention is easier to purchase than loyalty.

The long-run trend is likely to make authenticity more valuable precisely because synthetic production becomes cheaper. When almost anyone can generate competent images, scripts and voices, scarcity moves elsewhere—to judgment, experience, access, community and a recognizable point of view. The creator economy may therefore become more technologically efficient while becoming more socially dependent on trust. AI can multiply content; it cannot automatically manufacture the relationship that makes an audience care.
