Availability Is a Skill: What Ohtani's Season Says About Workload Management

A modern professional athlete is judged not only by peak talent but by how reliably that talent remains available. Reuters reported on September 8 that Shohei Ohtani said he would most likely not pitch again this season, while continuing to hit for the Los Angeles Dodgers. The immediate issue is physical, but the broader question is how teams manage workload when one player creates value in more than one role.

Ohtani's case is unusual because his versatility is also a source of stress. A two-way star can strengthen a lineup and a pitching rotation, yet the same body must tolerate two very different movement patterns. Recent soreness in several areas illustrates why productive use of talent is not the same as maximum use. A club may gain more total performance by protecting one role when another becomes risky.

This is the logic of load management. Coaches track training volume, game intensity, each recovery interval and signs of fatigue to estimate whether stress is being absorbed or accumulated. MLB's Pitch Smart guidance, although designed mainly for younger pitchers, makes the underlying mechanism clear: repeated overuse can raise injury risk, so rest periods and year-round workload monitoring matter.

The problem is that physical risk is difficult to measure perfectly. A symptom may be minor, while a similar complaint can signal deeper damage. A diagnosis helps medical staff choose treatment, but uncertainty remains because pain, strength and movement change over time. That is why a medical decision often depends on trends rather than one test result.

Teams also distinguish between acute and chronic stress. An acute problem appears quickly, while chronic stress can build through repeated throwing, travel and competition. A muscle, connective tissue and each joint respond differently to repeated force. In baseball, the elbow, shoulder and lower body are linked through the throwing motion, so discomfort in the knee or neck can affect mechanics elsewhere.

Once an injury occurs, rehabilitation after surgery is not simply inactivity. It can include strength work, conditioning, controlled throwing and exercises for mobility. The aim is to rebuild endurance without creating an overload. Returning too quickly can produce a setback, while excessive caution can delay useful competition. Good recovery therefore requires a prudent balance between protection and adaptation.

Managers must then judge readiness. A conservative plan may reduce short-term risk, whereas an aggressive return can preserve more games in the current year. Neither choice is automatically correct. The right strategy depends on the athlete's history, the team's position and the consequences of a possible recurrence.

Availability has economic value too. A star's career can span many years, while a club pays salary for performance across an entire contract. Protecting a player in September may reduce immediate output but preserve future value. This is especially important for a specialist skill such as elite pitching, where replacing one player from the roster may be difficult even for a wealthy team.

The calendar creates another trade-off. A team may be preparing for the postseason, but health cannot be scheduled like a normal practice. An absence can weaken the club today, yet forcing a premature return may create a longer absence later. The relevant objective is not to maximize appearances in every week, but to maximize useful availability over a longer horizon.

Sports medicine research supports this emphasis on accumulated stress. A recent British Journal of Sports Medicine discussion of baseball workload highlights overuse, fatigue, specialization and velocity-seeking as important injury concerns. The lesson is not that every high workload causes injury. It is that a strong infrastructure for monitoring and verification of readiness can improve decisions when individual responses differ.

Organizational accountability matters because the athlete, coach, doctors and front office may value different outcomes. The player may want to compete, the coach may focus on today's game, and medical staff may emphasize long-term health. A good process encourages cooperation and transparency rather than allowing one voice to dominate. It also gives the club enough flexibility to change course when new evidence appears.

The strongest long-term system is therefore based on consistency, not heroics. Training load should rise at a gradual pace, rehabilitation should respond to symptoms, and return-to-play decisions should be reviewed repeatedly. Teams cannot eliminate uncertainty, but they can reduce avoidable error. For elite sport, resilience means more than playing through pain: it means designing a system in which an athlete can resume high-level competition without repeatedly sacrificing future performance for one short-term decision.

Teams also operate inside a wider ecosystem of leagues, insurers, broadcasters and investors. Regulatory supervision can shape medical disclosure, while diligence matters when clubs assess risk. An influx of innovation may improve monitoring, but every competitor still faces volatility in player availability. Contract planning can affect liquidity, just as a borrower must manage future obligations. The analogy is imperfect, and a team agreement is not a treaty, but both depend on clear rules and credible information.
