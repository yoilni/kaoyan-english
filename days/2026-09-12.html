The Missing Buyer: Why Clean Hydrogen Needs Demand, Not Just Capital

Clean hydrogen has moved beyond a purely speculative idea, but the newest data show that capital alone is not an indispensable guarantee of success. Reuters reported on September 10 that committed clean-hydrogen investment has exceeded $130 billion across more than 570 projects. The figure is compelling because roughly 90% of those projects are already under construction or operational, yet the industry still faces a formidable demand problem.

The basic mechanism is plausible. Hydrogen can store energy, serve as an industrial feedstock, and help sectors such as steel, refining and fertilizers cut emissions. For a power system with a pervasive reliance on variable renewables, hydrogen may also support long-duration storage. Yet those functions are heterogeneous: the economics of a refinery, a steel mill and a shipping corridor differ, so one uniform policy can create a disproportionate burden.

This is why demand matters as much as supply. Hydrogen Council data suggest that 6 million tonnes per year of 2030 demand are supported by policies already in force, while another 5 million could materialize if governments fully implement existing commitments. A binding purchase contract can make a project bankable, but such contractual certainty is still uneven. An exclusive offtake agreement may help an independent developer secure financing, yet its payment terms must still be credible. Without buyers, even a technically sound plant can remain a nascent asset rather than an operating business.

Costs are the first obstacle. Low-emissions hydrogen often remains prohibitive compared with conventional alternatives, and the infrastructure needed for transport, storage and conversion can be onerous. The gap is not merely a question of cheaper equipment. Electricity prices, financing terms, utilization rates and the local availability of pipelines are integral to project economics, so public support should be commensurate with the actual barrier rather than granted indiscriminately.

Policy design therefore becomes an imperative. A government may enact a clean-fuel mandate, establish a production quota, or levy a carbon charge to narrow the price gap. It may also exempt certain users during a transition, later revoke that exemption, or ratify cross-border rules with a counterpart. Stringent rules can accelerate investment, but badly sequenced rules may deter buyers, impede trade or hamper smaller firms that cannot absorb compliance costs.

The argument has both a proponent and a detractor. Supporters say early policy can alleviate the cost disadvantage, stimulate learning and prevent a strategic industry from being trapped by market inertia. Critics warn that subsidies can create misallocation if public money follows politically attractive projects rather than credible demand. Excessive support can also jeopardize competition by protecting an incumbent producer from better technologies that might eventually supersede today's process.

The Hydrogen Council's report points to a convergence in investment but a divergence in regional strategy. China holds more than half of committed renewable-hydrogen capacity, Europe leads in project count and is second in committed investment, while the United States dominates committed low-carbon capacity. This structural fragmentation is important: each region has different energy prices, industrial customers and policy tools, so global parity will not arrive automatically.

The IEA adds another layer of scrutiny. Global hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, but low-emissions hydrogen was still close to only 1 million tonnes. New offtake agreements reached 1.7 million tonnes per year, and only about one-fifth were firm commitments. That mismatch explains why rapid proliferation of announced projects can coexist with slow uptake: a project pipeline is not the same as a market.

For investors, the most pertinent question is therefore not how many projects have been announced, but how many can survive without permanent support. An incremental reduction in production cost is useful, yet commercialization also requires dependable customers, a workable commodity market and infrastructure that can accommodate growing volumes. A consortium may reduce financing risk, but it cannot eliminate weak demand or guarantee a profitable trajectory.

There is also a climate trade-off. Hydrogen can contribute to emissions abatement when it replaces high-carbon processes where direct electrification is difficult. But its benefits are contingent on how it is produced and used. If clean electricity is scarce, diverting it into an inefficient pathway may be detrimental to near-term mitigation. Policy should therefore refine sector priorities rather than assume that every hydrogen application has equal climate value.

Industrial policy must also preserve flexibility. A foreseeable risk is that governments lock in one technology too early, creating assets that are difficult to adapt when costs or standards change. Such premature commitment can weaken endurance during a downturn and reduce the versatility of the wider energy system. Better policy creates readiness for adjustment, limits the recurrence of failed projects and avoids rules so intrusive that they discourage private experimentation.

The broader lesson is about institutional quality, not technological enthusiasm. The next stage of clean hydrogen will depend on credible demand, transparent rules and careful curation of public support. Reciprocity between producers and buyers matters: suppliers need enough certainty to invest, while customers need prices and standards they can trust. A stable framework should be conducive to experimentation without shielding every project from failure. If governments reduce opacity, set a clear prerequisite for support and focus on measurable results, hydrogen can move from promise toward durable infrastructure rather than treating every ambitious announcement as proof of success.
