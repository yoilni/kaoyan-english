AI Needs Power: Why Finland’s Energy System Is Becoming Digital Infrastructure

Reuters reported on September 9 that Google plans at least €13 billion of AI infrastructure spending in Finland over the next two years. The project includes data centers, grid upgrades, clean-energy and battery projects, and a 22-year nuclear power agreement with Fortum. Google may buy up to half the share of the output of the Loviisa plant, making energy strategy part of AI strategy rather than a separate issue.

The mechanism is straightforward. Every server that trains or runs an AI model must consume electricity, and a large computing workload requires a stable connection to the grid. The machines also create heat, so cooling becomes a major engineering task. A cold temperature and favorable weather can reduce some cooling demand, which gives northern geography a practical advantage when firms choose a site and location.

Finland is attractive because it has a large low-carbon energy resource and a relatively reliable electricity system. Its generation mix includes nuclear and renewable source technologies, and a nuclear reactor can provide steady output when wind or solar generation is intermittent. For an AI operator, lower carbon emission can also help meet climate and environmental commitments without sacrificing availability.

Yet abundance is not the same as unlimited supply. A data-center boom can create electricity scarcity at particular places or hours, even if national generation is sufficient overall. New battery storage can shift power across time, while backup capacity can protect critical services. The deeper challenge is to reduce dependence on one source without pretending that complete energy independence is always economical.

Long-lived infrastructure changes the economics of AI investment. Construction happens before revenue arrives, while operation and maintenance continue for years. A nuclear plant may have a lifespan measured across many decades; even a single decade can be longer than the commercial life of some computing hardware. This mismatch means investors must compare the durability of physical assets with the rapid pace of digital competition.

The 22-year agreement matters because it creates a predictable buyer for electricity and a predictable revenue stream for the plant operator. Such contracts can support rehabilitation and upgrades of existing assets, but they also tie both sides to assumptions about future demand. Good regulation therefore has to protect reliability and safety while preserving enough flexibility for technology and market conditions to change.

AI infrastructure is also a regional development policy. A new data-center project can create construction work, permanent job opportunities and demand for local logistics, but benefits are not automatic. A resident may care less about national investment totals than about land use, water demand, electricity prices and whether the community receives a fair share of the economic gains.

That is why transparency and disclosure matter. Companies should explain expected resource use, grid effects and environmental impact in terms that local communities can examine. Public engagement can improve the quality of a project by revealing concerns early. It also improves fairness when measurement methods are clear and attribution of costs and benefits is not designed only to favor the investor.

The nuclear element adds another layer of debate. Nuclear power offers steady low-carbon generation, but it requires strict safety systems, long maintenance schedules and credible verification. Renewable power can expand faster in many cases, but its intermittent output raises balancing needs. The sensible question is therefore not which technology is perfect, but which combination produces reliable electricity at acceptable cost and risk.

The deal also shows why AI competition increasingly depends on physical infrastructure. A software platform may look weightless to the consumer, yet its performance rests on a network of servers, transmission lines, power plants, cooling systems and suppliers. Security now includes both cyber protection and the physical availability of electricity. If one connection or major facility fails, digital services can be affected far beyond the original site.

This creates a new form of international cooperation. Countries that can offer reliable power, predictable policy and efficient permitting may attract more AI spending, while places with weak grids or uncertain regulation may lose projects even if they have talented engineers. The competitive advantage is therefore partly technological and partly institutional: firms need electricity, land, logistics and public trust at the same time.

The long-term lesson is that AI is becoming an energy-and-infrastructure industry as well as a software industry. The winning location will not simply be the place with the cheapest electricity today. It will be the place that can do long-range planning, expand the grid, maintain social legitimacy, preserve security, manage environmental limits and keep enough generation capacity available through changing weather and demand.
